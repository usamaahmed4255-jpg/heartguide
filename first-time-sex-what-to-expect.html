First Time Sex: What to Expect and How to Make It Good

First time sex is usually awkward, quick, and nothing like the movies — and that’s fine. What to expect, how to prepare, and how to make it good for both of you.

By Jordan Ellis · Deeper In · 11 min read · Last updated 11 November 2026

First time sex is one of the most talked-about things in the world and one of the least honestly described. The movies show it as perfect, or tragic, or both at once. Real first time sex is usually none of those things. It is awkward. It is quick. Something does not quite work the way you expected. And then it is over, and you lie there thinking, “Was that it?” That is normal. It is not a sign you did it wrong. It is a sign you are new at something, and new things are clumsy.

The good news is that a first time can still be a good time — not because it is perfect, but because it is honest, and kind, and unhurried. Here is what to actually expect, and how to make it good for both of you.

Forget the movies

In the films, first time sex is either fireworks or a disaster. In real life it is usually somewhere in between, and much quieter than either. It does not last very long. It might be a bit uncomfortable. One of you might laugh, or apologise, or go quiet. All of that is fine. The movies skip the awkward parts because awkward does not sell tickets. Real life has awkward parts, and they are not a problem. They are just part of it.

The most useful thing you can do before the first time is to lower your expectations on purpose. Not to zero — just to human. It will not be the best sex of your life. It does not need to be. It needs to be safe, kind, and something you both chose. Everything else gets better with practice, and you have plenty of time for that.

Talk first — really

Before anything happens, talk. Not a big formal speech. Just an honest conversation, when you are both dressed and calm, about what you want and what you do not want. Are you both sure? Is there anything either of you is nervous about? What protection are you using? These are not mood-killers. They are the reason the mood can be relaxed later, because neither of you is worrying about the things you left unsaid.

Bring protection, and talk about it before, not during. No exceptions, no “it will be fine.” Being prepared is not unromantic. It is the most caring thing you can do.

Consent is not a form you sign once. It is checking in, all the way through. “Is this okay?” “Do you want to keep going?” “Should we slow down?” Asking does not ruin the moment. It makes the moment safe, and safe is what lets both of you relax enough to enjoy it.

Slow is the whole trick

If there is one piece of advice for a first time, it is this: go slow. Slower than you think. Spend a long time on everything before the main event — kissing, touching, just being close. Rushing is the cause of almost every bad first time. It makes things uncomfortable, it makes people nervous, and it skips the part that actually feels good.

  • Take your time kissing. A long time. There is no clock.
  • Check in as you go: “Is this good?” “Do you like this?”
  • If something feels wrong, stop. Stopping is always allowed.
  • Breathe. Slow breaths calm you both down.
  • There is no finish line. You do not have to get anywhere.

The goal is not to perform. The goal is to be together, and to feel good about it afterwards. Slow is how you get there. Slow is not a consolation prize. Slow is the whole trick.

If it hurts

For some people, especially women, the first time can hurt a bit. That is common, and it is usually because of tension, not enough time, or not enough lubrication. The fix is more of everything slow: more time, more touching first, more patience. If it hurts, stop. Do not push through pain to get it over with. Pain is your body saying not yet, and listening to it is the right thing to do.

If it keeps hurting, time after time, that is worth talking to a doctor about. There are real, common reasons for ongoing pain, and they are treatable. It is not something to be embarrassed about, and it is not something to just live with. A doctor has heard it a thousand times before.

After is part of it

What happens after matters as much as what happens during. Do not get up and leave. Do not grab your phone. Stay. Hold each other. Talk, or be quiet together — either is fine. The first time can bring up a lot of feelings, and they are not always the ones you expected. Some people feel happy. Some people feel shy. Some people feel a bit sad, or strange, and they do not know why. All of that is normal.

And afterwards, in the days that follow, check in with each other. “How are you feeling about it?” is a good question. The first time is not a test you pass or fail. It is the start of something you figure out together, and the figuring out is the good part.

Questions people ask

Will it bleed? Sometimes, a little. Sometimes not at all. Bleeding is not a test of anything, and not bleeding does not mean anything either. If there is a little blood, it is normal and it stops on its own. If there is a lot, or it does not stop, talk to a doctor.

What if it doesn’t work? Then it doesn’t work this time, and you try again another time. Bodies are not machines. Nerves, tiredness, too much to drink — all of it can get in the way. It is not a failure. It is a first try. Most first tries do not go perfectly, in anything.

How do I know I’m ready? You are ready when you want to, not when someone else wants you to, and not because everyone else seems to have done it. If you are doing it because you feel pressured, you are not ready. If you are doing it because you genuinely want to, with someone you trust, that is ready enough.
