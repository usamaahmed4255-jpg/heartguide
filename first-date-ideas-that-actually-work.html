First Date Ideas That Actually Work

A good first date gives you something to talk about and an easy way out. Here are ideas that do both, for every budget.

By Jordan Ellis · First Dates & Early Days · 11 min read · Last updated 10 November 2026

In this guide

  1. Start with a walk, not a table
  2. Give your hands something to do
  3. Eat somewhere you can actually talk
  4. Skip the cinema (and the fancy dinner)
  5. Questions people ask

I once sat through a two-hour dinner where we ran out of things to say before the starters arrived. Two decent people, one very long silence, and a waiter who kept refilling water nobody wanted. It was not a lack of chemistry. It was a bad setup. A table, two chairs, nothing to look at except each other. That is a job interview, not a date.

A good first date does two jobs. It hands you something to talk about, and it hands you an easy exit after an hour if the spark is not there. Everything below does both. No reservations, no dress code, no three-course commitment. Just an hour, a bit of fresh air, and a plan.

Start with a walk, not a table

Grab a coffee and walk. A park loop, a river path, a street with good shop windows — anywhere you can move side by side instead of staring at each other across a table. Walking takes the pressure off eye contact. The conversation just flows better when your feet are moving, and when it stalls for a second, there is a dog, a busker, a weird shop sign to comment on.

Pick a route with a clear end. A forty-minute loop is perfect. If it is going well, suggest a second stop. If it is not, the walk ends on its own and nobody has to fake a headache. And name the place when you ask. “The blue café on Elm Street, six o’clock Tuesday?” is a plan. “Let’s grab coffee sometime” is homework you have handed to a stranger.

Give your hands something to do

Here is the thing about activity dates: the silence never feels awkward, because you are both busy. A pottery class where your mug collapses. A round of mini golf where you both miss the easiest hole. A wander round a farmers’ market, tasting things you cannot pronounce. You will remember the terrible pot you made long after you forget what you ordered for dinner.

  • Mini golf or bowling: light competition, lots of laughing, easy to leave after one game.
  • A market or food hall: wander, taste things, split a few small plates between you.
  • A short class: pottery, painting, cooking — somewhere messing up is half the fun.
  • One small exhibition: not the whole museum. Pick a room, talk about what you actually like.
  • A dog walk: if one of you has a dog, bring them. Dogs break the ice faster than any question.

Keep it short and cheap. Two hours is plenty. A full-day hike or a four-hour class is too much for someone you have just met — that is a shared ordeal, not a shared laugh. Look for places used to beginners, so neither of you feels judged. A bookshop browse works the same way. Pull something strange off a shelf, ask what they think. You learn more from that than from a list of sensible questions.

Eat somewhere you can actually talk

Dinner is not banned. It just works better when it is casual. A taco truck. A pizza place where you order at the counter. A noodle bar with communal tables. You can talk normally, the bill is small, and nobody feels trapped by a three-course meal and a waiter hovering with the dessert menu.

Try this when you suggest it: “There’s a taco place on 5th Street that does great al pastor — want to meet there Thursday at 7? We can keep it to an hour if you’ve got an early morning.”

Order food you can eat while talking. Spaghetti that needs both hands is a bad choice. So is anything so spicy you spend the date coughing into a napkin. Shareable plates help — splitting two or three small dishes gives you something to discuss and makes the meal feel like a team effort instead of a formal event. If you suggested the place, get there five minutes early, grab a table, and text them where you are sitting. Small kindness. Sets the whole evening at ease.

Skip the cinema (and the fancy dinner)

A film is the worst first date going. Two hours in the dark, saying nothing, then having to manufacture a conversation about a film one of you did not like. Save the cinema for date three, when you already know you enjoy talking to each other. Same for a concert, a play, anything where you cannot hear each other properly. You came to find out if you like this person. You cannot do that in silence.

Skip anything expensive or all-day, too. A fancy dinner, a weekend trip, a cooking class that runs four hours — all of it puts pressure on a first meeting that it does not need. If the date goes badly, you have wasted an evening and a chunk of money. If it goes well, you will wish you had saved the big gesture for later, when it means something. Keep the first one simple enough that a second one feels like a step up. And forget the interview questions — nobody needs your five-year plan before dessert. Ask about the trip they still talk about, the hobby they spend too much money on, the show they have watched three times. How they answer tells you more than what they answer.

Questions people ask

How long should a first date last? An hour to ninety minutes. Long enough to know if you want a second one, short enough to leave while you are still enjoying it. If it is going brilliantly, extend it. Ending early on a high note beats dragging on until the energy fades.

Who should pay? Whoever suggested the date should offer, and the other person should offer to split. Keep it easy. If they insist on splitting, let them. If they insist on paying, say thank you and get the next round.

What if we run out of things to say? Look around you. Comment on the food, the street, the terrible painting on the wall. Ask a follow-up about something they already mentioned instead of jumping to a new topic. And if the silence keeps coming back, that is useful information too — chemistry is partly about how comfortable the quiet feels.
