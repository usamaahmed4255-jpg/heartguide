How to Handle Ghosting

One day they're there, the next day they're gone. Ghosting says everything about them and nothing about you — but it still stings. Here's how to deal with it.

By Jordan Ellis · First Dates & Early Days · 11 min read · Last updated 10 November 2026

In this guide

  1. Stop hunting for the why
  2. One message. Then stop.
  3. You're allowed to be annoyed
  4. When they do it again
  5. Getting past it, properly
  6. Questions people ask

Three good dates. Warm texts. The last one ended with a hug and a plan for Friday. Then nothing. No reply Thursday. No reply Friday. By Sunday you are staring at your phone, re-reading your last message for a typo, wondering what you did wrong.

You did nothing wrong. It happens to kind, funny, attractive people all the time. It hurts because the story is left unfinished — no ending, no explanation, just silence where a person used to be. Your brain hates that gap and fills it with worst-case theories about you. This is about closing the gap yourself, without chasing someone who handles difficult conversations by running away from them.

Stop hunting for the why

The first instinct is to find out why. Was it something I said? Was the second date too keen? Did they meet someone else? The urge makes sense, but the search has no good ending. Even if they answered, it would probably be vague — “I’ve just been really busy.” That tells you nothing, and it still leaves you waiting for a person who could not send a two-line message.

Most of the time, the reason has little to do with you. People ghost because they are avoidant, because they met someone else, because they got back with an ex, or because ending it felt like too much effort. None of that is about your worth. Give it a deadline: someone who normally replies within a day, gone quiet for three or four days with no warning, is ghosting, not busy. Busy people send a short message. People who have disappeared do not.

One message. Then stop.

You get one final message. Not five. Not a paragraph at 2 a.m. One calm check-in, short and warm, no blame. Say you noticed, you are not angry, the ball is in their court. Then put the phone down and mean it.

Try this: “Hey, I’ve not heard from you in a few days, so I’m guessing the timing isn’t right. No hard feelings at all — if you want to pick this up again, you know where I am. Take care.”

After that, stop. No follow-up, no liking their photos to get their attention. Every extra message hands them more power over your mood. One message is mature and clear. Five is a spiral you will regret in the morning. If they reply with a real apology and a plan, you can decide then. If they do not, you have your answer — and you gave yourself a proper ending.

You're allowed to be annoyed

People will tell you not to take it personally, and in the long run they are right. In the short run, you are allowed to be annoyed. It is rude. You gave someone your time, you showed up, you were honest, and they could not even send a short goodbye. Being angry about that is a sign you know how you deserve to be treated.

  • Tell one friend: say it out loud once, get the eye-roll and the support, then move on.
  • Move your body: a walk, a swim, a gym session — anything that breaks the phone-staring loop.
  • Remove the trigger: mute or unfollow them for now; you can always change it later.
  • Write it out: a private note, not a message to them, gets the frustration out of your head.
  • Do one normal thing: cook a proper dinner, see a friend, watch something funny.

Let the feeling have its hour, not its week. Talk to a friend, go for a run, write the angry message in your notes app and never send it. What does not help is sitting alone refreshing their profile and building a case against yourself. That loop turns a small rejection into a verdict on your whole dating life. It is not. It is one person, one bad habit, one closed door.

When they do it again

Ghosted once, that is bad luck. Ghosted twice by the same person, that is a pattern. This is the person who disappears for two weeks, then pops back with a casual “hey stranger” as if nothing happened. They are keeping you as a backup — someone to text when they are bored, with no intention of showing up properly.

Do not reward the return. Someone who ghosted you once and comes back without an apology has not changed. They have just got bored. If you reply warmly and pick up where you left off, you teach them disappearing has no cost. Leave it unanswered, or reply once, briefly: “I’m not really up for starting and stopping like this. I wish you well.” That is enough. And if it keeps happening with different people, ask one honest question — are you moving too fast, or picking people who were lukewarm from the start?

Getting past it, properly

Moving on does not mean pretending it did not hurt, or jumping straight back onto the apps to prove you are fine. It means getting your normal life back first. See your friends. Do the things you enjoy. Let a week pass where you do not think about them at all. When the sting fades on its own, you are actually over it, not just distracted.

Then, when you are ready, date again — but keep one lesson. Look for people who communicate, even when it is awkward. The person who says “I had a nice time, but I don’t think we’re a match” is worth ten people who vanish. That small honesty tells you everything about how they will handle harder conversations later. Ghosting is a filter, in a painful way. It removes people who cannot do the basics. And remember: how someone ends things matters as much as how they start them. You now know what you will not accept. That is not bitterness. That is a standard.

Questions people ask

How long before I know I’ve been ghosted? If they normally reply within a day and you have heard nothing for three or four days, that is it. A busy person sends a short holding message. Silence for a week is a decision.

Should I confront them if I see them in person? No big scene. A brief, calm “I noticed you went quiet, I hope you’re alright” is plenty if you bump into them. You do not need a public showdown to get closure. Their silence already gave it to you.

What if they come back months later? Take it seriously only if it is specific — what happened, why they vanished, what would be different now. A vague “hey, how are you?” after months of silence is not an apology. It is a test to see if the door is still open. You decide if it is.
