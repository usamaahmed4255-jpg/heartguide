How to Tell If Someone Likes You

Nobody sends a signed letter. Interest shows up in small, repeated things — here's what to actually look for.

By Jordan Ellis · First Dates & Early Days · 12 min read · Last updated 10 November 2026

In this guide

  2. The small stuff they remember
  3. What their body is doing (and what it isn't)
  4. Sometimes it's just being nice

We have all done the detective work. Re-reading “haha yeah” six times. Asking a friend what a full stop really means. Wondering if a smile at work was just a smile. Here is the less dramatic truth: people who like you do not send one perfect signal. They do a lot of small, ordinary things, again and again. The pattern gives them away. One long reply means nothing. One compliment means nothing. But when someone keeps showing up, keeps remembering, and keeps finding reasons to be near you — pay attention.

Do they actually find the time?

Time costs something, which is why it is the clearest signal there is. A person who likes you will find time even when their week is full. They suggest a specific day instead of “we should hang out sometime.” They reply without leaving you on read for two days, and when life does get in the way, they come back with a real explanation and a new plan, not just silence.

Watch for effort, not just availability. Someone free every Friday who invites you along is being friendly. Someone who leaves a work event early, or drives twenty minutes to see you for half an hour, is showing interest. Repeated choices add up. And notice who starts things — if you are always texting first, it may be one-sided. A person who likes you reaches out too, with a meme that reminded them of you or a “how did the interview go?” on Wednesday morning.

Listen for this: “You said you liked that bakery on Mill Road — I walked past it today and thought of you. Want to go Saturday?” Memory, effort, and a plan, all in one message.

The small stuff they remember

Most people forget the small stuff. Someone who likes you does not. They remember you hate coriander, your sister lives in Bristol, you have a big presentation on Wednesday — then they bring it up later, unprompted. “How did the presentation go?” is a small sentence that says a lot. It means they were listening, and they kept it in mind after the conversation ended.

This is hard to fake. You can force a quick reply or a longer smile, but remembering that someone mentioned their mum’s birthday three weeks ago takes genuine attention. Look for follow-ups — do they ask about the thing you were worried about, or order your usual without asking? Those are not accidents.

What their body is doing (and what it isn't)

Body language gets talked about far too much, and most of it is overblown. Nobody can read minds from crossed arms. A few things are worth noticing, though, especially when they happen together. A person who likes you will often turn their whole body towards you in a group, not just their head. Hold eye contact a beat longer than normal, then look away and smile. Find small excuses to be close — sitting next to you when other seats are free, leaning in when you talk, touching your arm briefly when they laugh.

None of that proves anything alone. Some people are warm and touchy with everyone; some are shy and barely look at the person they fancy. Treat body language as supporting evidence, not the whole case. Leaning in, laughing at your worst jokes, texting first, remembering your coffee order — together, that picture is clear. Standing near you once at a party is not.

Sometimes it's just being nice

Here is the part people skip. A lot of friendly behaviour is just that — friendly. A colleague who is kind to everyone, a barista who remembers your name, a friend who hugs you hello. None of that means they want to date you. Warm people exist, and mistaking their warmth for interest leads to awkward conversations and hurt feelings.

  • Politeness: replies when you message, is kind in person, treats everyone the same way.
  • Interest: messages first, suggests meeting up, remembers details, treats you differently.
  • Mixed signals: warm one week, distant the next — usually unsure, busy, or seeing someone else.
  • Clear disinterest: short replies, no follow-up questions, always too busy, never suggests a plan.

The test is simple: do they treat you differently from other people? If they are flirty with everyone in the room, that is their personality, not a signal aimed at you. If they are normally quiet, but they light up around you and linger after everyone else has left — that difference matters.

So — what do you do about it?

You have spotted the pattern. They make time, they remember things, they light up when you walk in. Now what? Make it easy for them to say yes. No grand confession needed. Just a low-pressure invitation with a clear way to accept. Coffee on Saturday morning. A walk after work. The new exhibition at the gallery near their flat. A clear plan beats a vague “we should do something sometime” every time.

Still unsure? Give them one more opening and watch what happens. Mention a film you want to see and see if they suggest going together. Say you are trying a new restaurant this weekend and leave a pause. Interested people fill that pause — “can I come?” People who are not interested wish you a nice time and change the subject. Either way, you have your answer without an awkward speech. And if you get it wrong, it stings, but it is survivable. The worst outcome is not rejection. It is spending six months wondering and never asking.

Questions people ask

What if they like all my photos but never message? Liking photos is low effort — it might mean interest, or it might just mean they like your photos. Reply to one of their stories with a real comment, not just an emoji, and see if a conversation starts. If it fizzles after one exchange, you have your answer.

Can someone like you and still take hours to reply? Yes, easily. Some people are bad at texting, busy at work, or just not glued to their phone. Judge the pattern over a week, not a single afternoon. Do they always come back? Do they ask questions when they do? That matters far more than speed.

How do I know it is not just in my head? Ask a friend who has seen you together. They will spot the difference in how someone acts around you far faster than you will, because you are too close to it. And if three separate signs all point the same way — time, memory, attention — trust the pattern, not the doubt.
