How to Keep the Spark Alive in a Long-Distance Relationship

Distance doesn't kill relationships — routine and silence do. Small, regular things beat grand gestures every time.

By Jordan Ellis · Deeper In · 12 min read · Last updated 10 November 2026

In this guide

  2. Stop saving up the boring bits
  3. Book the next visit before this one ends
  4. Say the awkward thing early
  5. Questions people ask

Most long-distance couples don't fall apart in one big fight. They go quiet. One week it's just a missed call, then the good-morning texts stop, then suddenly you're two people who used to talk every day and now send a meme on Sunday and call that enough. Nobody chose that. Life filled the gap first.

The couples who last aren't the grand-gesture ones. They're the ones with a small rhythm they actually keep — a Sunday call that happens even when they're tired, a shared show, a plan for the next time they'll be in the same room. None of it looks romantic on its own. Together, it keeps you inside each other's ordinary days, which is where a relationship really lives.

The Tuesday 8pm thing

Pick something that happens at the same time every week, and guard it like an appointment. Tuesday at 8pm, you watch the same episode and text through it. Friday, you cook the same recipe in your own kitchens — his pasta always comes out wetter than hers, and that's now a running joke. One chapter of the same book a week, argued over on Sunday.

The activity barely matters. What matters is that a slot in the week belongs to the two of you. When work runs late or a friend invites you out, you know what you're moving, not just vaguely "talking later." And keep one ritual off a screen: a letter once a month, a parcel with their favourite biscuits, a playlist you add one song to each week. A letter on the bedside table is still there on the days a video call feels like too much.

If a ritual starts feeling like a chore, kill it and try another. The test is simple. Does thinking about it make you smile, or sigh? Keep the first kind.

Stop saving up the boring bits

When you only get an hour a day, it's tempting to make every minute count — big feelings, big news, big plans. That gets exhausting fast. Real closeness is the boring stuff: the rude man at the pharmacy, the sandwich that cost £7 and tasted of nothing, your boss saying "quick chat?" at 4:55 on a Friday.

"Made your terrible pasta tonight. Still better than the takeaway place on my street. Miss you — call when you're done with work?"

Send those things as they happen. A photo of a funny sign on your walk home. A two-line voice note about a meeting that went badly. That does more than a long catch-up call, because your partner feels like they were with you during the day, not just hearing a report afterwards.

Don't turn it into a job, though. You don't need to reply instantly or narrate every hour. A few messages through the day, one proper conversation when you can both focus. And if you're swamped, say so plainly — "busy till 6, call you then" beats silence and guessing every time.

Book the next visit before this one ends

On the last night of a visit, before the goodbye gets heavy, pick the next date. Even a rough one. A saved flight search and "the 14th, probably" changes the hard weeks completely — you're not waiting vaguely, you're waiting until the 14th.

Plan one or two things, then leave the rest open. A visit crammed with sightseeing feels like a holiday with a stranger. A visit with one good plan — the farmers' market on Saturday, a film on Friday — and lots of ordinary time feels like a life. Cook breakfast. Do the laundry. Walk to the coffee shop on the corner. Those plain hours are the ones you'll miss.

  • Talk money early. Who pays for the train, who pays for dinner — say it before resentment does the talking, especially if one of you travels more.
  • Take an hour apart. A whole weekend glued together is a lot. A run, a book, a walk on your own is normal, not rejection.
  • Meet their people. Dinner with their friends, a coffee with their mum. It stops your partner being a secret from the rest of your life.
  • Plan the goodbye. Who's driving to the station, when's the first call after. A small plan makes the last evening easier than pretending it isn't happening.
  • Keep a shared list. Restaurants to try, films to watch, streets to walk. The next visit starts itself.

Say the awkward thing early

Feeling jealous? Lonely? Unsure about the future? Say it while it's small. "I've felt a bit left out this week" is a conversation. The same feeling after three months of silence is a fight.

Pick the moment, though. Not by text at midnight, not five minutes before a work meeting. "Can we talk properly tonight?" — then both show up for it. On a call, slow down. Leave pauses. Ask what they heard, not just what you said. Misunderstandings grow fast when you can't see someone's face properly.

And talk about the end of the distance, even if the answer is "not yet." Who might move, and when? What has to change first — a job, a lease, a visa? You don't need a fixed date. You need a direction. Couples can handle a long wait when they both know what they're waiting for. What wears people down is not knowing whether the wait ends at all.

Questions people ask

How often should we talk? As often as feels good to both of you, and no more. For a lot of couples that's a few messages a day and a proper call most evenings, but the number matters less than the pattern. Regular beats constant. If talking every day starts feeling like a duty, say so and agree a lighter rhythm instead of letting it curdle.

Does it work if we've never lived in the same place? Yes, but visits matter even more. You need real time together — ordinary days, not just holidays — to learn how the other person actually lives. Plan longer visits when you can, and be honest if what you learn surprises you. That's what the visits are for.

What if one of us gets jealous more easily? Jealousy usually points to a need underneath: more reassurance, clearer plans, more time together. Talk about that need, not the feeling itself. Agree small, concrete things — a text when plans change, a call after a night out — and keep them. Trust grows from kept promises, not from being told not to worry.
