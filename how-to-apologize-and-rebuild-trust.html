How to Apologize and Rebuild Trust

A real apology has no 'but' in it. Rebuilding trust takes longer than the apology — here's what the slow part looks like.

By Jordan Ellis · Deeper In · 12 min read · Last updated 10 November 2026

In this guide

  1. Sorry, without the "but"
  2. Let them ask it twice
  3. Boring reliability, for months
  4. When sorry isn't enough
  5. Questions people ask

You've said sorry. You meant it. And your partner is still hurt. That doesn't always mean the apology was fake — it usually means the apology was only the first step, and the rest of the work hasn't started yet. Saying sorry is a moment. Rebuilding trust is a season.

This is for the person who did the damage and wants to put it right, and for the person who was hurt and is trying to work out whether the apology in front of them is real. A good apology doesn't demand instant forgiveness, and a hurt partner doesn't owe a quick reset just because the words sounded right.

Sorry, without the "but"

Name what you did, in plain language. Not "I'm sorry you feel that way." Not "I'm sorry if I hurt you." Those put the problem on the other person's feelings. Try the harder version instead: "I lied about where I was on Friday. That was wrong, and I understand why it scared you." Specific. No softening.

Watch the word "but." "I'm sorry, but I was stressed" cancels the first half of the sentence. So does "I'm sorry, but you also…" If you need to explain the context, do it later, in a separate conversation, after the apology has landed. In the apology itself: what happened, why it was wrong, what you'll do differently. That's it.

And don't apologize just to end the conversation. A lot of apologies are really a request — please stop being upset now. Your partner can tell. Sit with the discomfort for a bit. Listen without correcting their version. If you need an hour to think before you can do that properly, say so: "I need to think about this and I'll come back tonight." Then come back. A delayed, honest apology beats a fast, empty one.

Let them ask it twice

After the apology, expect questions. "Why did you do it?" "Did you think about me?" "Has this happened before?" They'll ask the same one twice, maybe three times, on different days. That isn't an attack. It's someone trying to make sense of something that shook them.

  • Don't rush the second talk. The real feelings often come out days later, once the shock has faded. Make room for that conversation instead of acting surprised it's happening again.
  • Agree what changes. More openness about plans, a pause on a friendship that crossed a line, a regular check-in for a while. Write it down if it helps, so nobody has to guess later.
  • Let the silence sit. After you've answered, don't fill every pause with another explanation. Sometimes the kindest thing is to stop talking and let them think.
  • Check what they heard. "What did you take from that?" catches misunderstandings before they harden into a new fight.

Boring reliability, for months

Trust comes back in small, unremarkable moments. You said you'd call at seven and you did. You were where you said you'd be. You told the truth about something small when a lie would have been easier. Nobody throws a party for those moments. Week after week, though, they form a new pattern your partner can lean on.

That also means it can be undone fast. One more secret, one more broken promise, and you're back at the start — or further back, because now it looks like a pattern. So be boringly reliable for a while. On time. Doing what you said you'd do. Replying when you said you would. Reliability isn't romantic, and right now it's exactly what's needed.

Expect bad days. Your partner may seem fine for weeks, then a song, a street, a memory sets it off again. That's normal. Don't sigh. Don't say "haven't we moved past this?" after two weeks — you're rushing, and trust doesn't run on your timetable.

Be open without being asked. Share your plans, your whereabouts, your phone freely for a while. Volunteered honesty feels very different from honesty under questioning.

When sorry isn't enough

Some damage can't be fixed with a good apology and better behaviour, and it's kinder to say that plainly. If trust has been broken again and again — repeated lies, repeated cheating, the same promise made and broken in a cycle — the problem is bigger than one apology can carry. Professional counselling is the honest next step then, not another promise to try harder. A couples therapist can help you both see the pattern and decide together whether the relationship can be rebuilt safely.

"I broke our agreement when I texted her again. I won't do that again, and I know saying that isn't enough — I'll show you, starting with being honest about my phone."

Infidelity sits in that category too. Couples do work through it, but it usually needs outside help, time, and a real willingness from both people to look at what led there. If your partner is asking for therapy and you're refusing, that refusal is itself an answer.

Sometimes the honest outcome is that it ends. That doesn't mean the apology failed. It can mean both people were honest enough to see that trust can't be rebuilt, or that one person doesn't want to try. An apology doesn't buy a second chance. It offers one, and the other person is free to say no. If that happens, respect it — let them go without pressure or repeated attempts to change their mind. If you're the one who was hurt, you don't have to decide today. Talk to a friend you trust, or a counsellor on your own, and see how the next few weeks feel.

Questions people ask

How long does it take? Longer than most people hope — usually months, sometimes more, depending on what happened. There's no fixed timeline, and trying hard doesn't speed it up. What helps is consistency: the same honest behaviour, repeated, until it becomes the new normal.

Should I apologize again if they bring it up months later? In a way, yes. You don't need the full speech every time, but don't sigh or say "we've already talked about this." A short, steady answer works: "I know, I'm sorry, and I understand why it still hurts." Being patient with the aftershocks is part of the repair.

What if I apologized and they won't forgive me? That's their right. Keep showing up reliably, offer therapy, be honest about what's changed. What you can't do is demand forgiveness or punish them for not giving it. If it can't recover, the respectful move is to accept that and part as kindly as you can.
