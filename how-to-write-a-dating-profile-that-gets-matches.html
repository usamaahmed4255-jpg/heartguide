Starting Out

How to Write a Dating Profile That Gets Matches

Your profile is not a CV and not a confession — it's a short, honest invitation. Here's how to write one that sounds like you on a good day.

By Jordan Ellis · Starting Out · 12 min read · Last updated 10 November 2026

  2. The bio in three lines
  3. Say what you actually want
  4. Things that quietly put people off
  5. Keep it fresh

Think about the last time you swiped through profiles yourself. You probably made most of your decisions in about three seconds, based on a photo and a line or two of text. That is not shallow — it is just how the format works. A dating profile is a first impression squeezed into a phone screen, and the people reading yours are doing exactly what you do: looking for a reason to stop and look closer.

The good news is that you do not need to be the most attractive or the funniest person on the app. You need a profile that feels like a real person, gives someone something to ask you about, and does not make them work to figure out who you are. This guide walks through how to do that, photo by photo and line by line.

Photos do the heavy lifting

Photos matter more than anything else you write, so start there. Use five or six, and make the first one a clear, recent picture of your face in daylight. Not a group shot where someone has to guess which one you are. Not sunglasses and a hat. Not a photo from five years ago. Just you, looking like you do on a normal good day.

After that, show your life. One photo doing something you enjoy — cooking, hiking, playing guitar, walking the dog. One with friends, so people can see you have a social life. One that shows your sense of humour, if you have a photo that does that naturally. A travel photo is fine, but one is enough. Six pictures of you in front of famous landmarks starts to look like a holiday brochure.

The bio in three lines

Your bio should be short enough to read in one glance. Three lines is about right. The first line says something true about you. The second says something specific — a hobby, a habit, a small detail. The third gives the reader an easy way in, like a question or a playful invitation.

Specific always beats generic. "I love travel, food, and music" could describe almost anyone, and it gives a stranger nothing to reply to. "I make a decent Sunday roast, I am learning Italian badly, and I will defend my local pub quiz team to the death" is a person. Someone reading that can already imagine three different messages to send you.

"Weekend chef, terrible dancer, loyal to one coffee shop on Holloway Road. Tell me your best terrible joke and I'll tell you mine."

Say what you actually want

People respect honesty about intentions far more than they mind a mismatch. If you want something serious, say so — plainly, without apologising for it. "Looking for something real, and happy to take it slow" tells the right people exactly what they need to know. If you are not sure yet, that is fine too. "Open to seeing where things go" is honest, and it is kinder than pretending.

What does not work is saying nothing and hoping the other person guesses. It wastes everyone's time, and it tends to attract people who want something different from you. A clear line in your profile acts like a filter. Some people will swipe past, and that is the point. The ones who stay are the ones who actually want the same thing.

Things that quietly put people off

Some profile habits push people away without the writer realising it. They are rarely dramatic. They are small things that add up to a feeling of "this might be hard work" — and on an app, that feeling is enough to make someone swipe left.

  • Negativity: "No drama, no time-wasters" sounds tired before the conversation starts.
  • Empty prompts: Leaving every prompt blank suggests you could not be bothered.
  • Group-photo openers: If the first photo has four people in it, nobody knows who you are.
  • Lists of demands: "Must be over 6ft, must love dogs, must not..." reads like a job advert.
  • Outdated photos: A photo from years ago sets up an awkward first meeting for both of you.

None of these make you a bad person. They just make your profile harder to like. Swap the negative line for something you enjoy. Fill in two prompts properly. Put yourself first, alone, in the opening photo. Small fixes, big difference.

Keep it fresh

A profile is not a one-time job. Update it every few weeks, even in small ways. Swap in a new photo from a recent weekend. Change one prompt answer. Add a line about something you just started — a cooking class, a book you are halfway through, a farmers' market you found on Saturday morning. Fresh details give people new reasons to message you, and they show you are actually active rather than a profile someone set up months ago and forgot.

It is also worth re-reading your bio after a few weeks with fresh eyes. Does it still sound like you? Has anything changed? Maybe you have a new favourite café, or you finally finished that DIY project in the kitchen. Profiles that change a little over time feel alive. Profiles that sit untouched for a year feel like a shop window with the lights off.

Questions people ask

How many photos should I use? Five or six is the sweet spot. Fewer than three feels thin, and more than eight starts to feel like a slideshow. Make sure the first one is a clear, recent photo of just you, and that at least one shows you doing something you enjoy.

Should I mention my job? Yes, briefly, if it is part of your life — but it does not need to be the headline. "Teacher, weekend cyclist" tells someone more than a job title alone. What matters is the person behind the job, not the job itself.

What if I am not photogenic? Most people feel that way about themselves. Daylight, a relaxed expression, and a photo taken by someone else usually beat a stiff selfie. Ask a friend to take a few while you are doing something normal, like making coffee or walking in the park. Candid photos almost always look better than posed ones.
