How to Talk About Sex With Your Partner (Without Making It Awkward)

Most couples find sex harder to talk about than to have. Here is how to start the conversation without it turning into a fight or a joke.

By Jordan Ellis · Deeper In · 11 min read · Last updated 11 November 2026

In this guide

  1. Say it outside the bedroom
  2. Start with what you like
  3. Use “I”, not “you”
  4. Ask one question, then listen
  5. When they go quiet
  6. Questions people ask

You can share a bed with someone for years and still never have said, out loud, what you actually like. You will talk about money, in-laws, whose turn it is to do the dishes — but the thing you do together in the dark stays off limits. Most couples are like this. It is not a sign your relationship is broken. It is a sign nobody ever taught you how to have this conversation.

The awkwardness is normal, and it passes faster than you think. The first time your voice might shake a bit. The second time it is easier. By the third time it is just a conversation. This guide is about getting through the first one without a fight, a joke to cover the nerves, or a silence that lasts a week.

Say it outside the bedroom

Do not start this conversation in bed, during sex, or right after it. That is the worst possible moment. In bed, everything sounds like a verdict. A suggestion becomes a complaint, a question becomes an accusation, and nobody is thinking clearly anyway. Pick a normal moment instead. A walk. A drive. Cooking dinner together. Somewhere you are side by side, not face to face. It is much easier to say something honest when you are not staring at each other waiting for a reaction. Pick a calm day, not one you have just argued on.

You do not need a big announcement either. “Can we talk about our sex life?” sounds like a meeting nobody wants to attend. Just start small, in the middle of an ordinary moment. “I was thinking about us the other day...” is enough. Outside the bedroom, with no pressure, both of you can actually hear each other.

Start with what you like

If the first thing out of your mouth is a complaint, the conversation is over before it starts. Nobody hears “I wish you did this differently” as helpful feedback in the moment. They hear “you are doing it wrong.” So start with what you already like. “I love it when you...” is the easiest sentence in this whole subject, and most people never say it. Say it first, and mean it. Then the thing you want to change lands differently — not a list of failures, but a person who likes their partner and wants more of the good stuff.

Try this: “I love it when we take our time in the morning. Could we do that more often — the slow mornings, not the rushed ones?”

This is not about being dishonest or hiding what you want. It is about order. Good news first, then the ask. The ask itself should be small and specific, not a whole review of your sex life in one go. One thing. “Could we try...” beats “We need to talk about everything.” One change at a time is how this actually works.

Use “I”, not “you”

The fastest way to turn a sex talk into a fight is starting sentences with “you”. “You never...” and “You always...” put the other person on trial, and people on trial stop listening. Flip it around. “I feel...” and “I would love...” say the same thing without pointing a finger. “I miss feeling close to you” is a completely different sentence from “You never touch me anymore.” One opens a door. The other slams it.

  • “You never initiate” → “I love it when you start things — it makes me feel wanted.”
  • “You’re always tired” → “I miss our time together. Can we protect one evening a week?”
  • “You don’t like what I like” → “There is something I would love to try with you. Can I tell you about it?”
  • “It’s boring now” → “I want us to have more fun together. What sounds good to you?”

None of these are magic words. They just leave room for the other person to answer instead of defend themselves. Say what you feel and what you want, and leave the blame out of it.

Ask one question, then listen

Most sex talks fail because one person does all the talking and the other one waits for it to be over. Ask a real question, then stop and actually listen to the answer. Not “is everything fine?” — that just gets a yes. Ask something that needs a real answer. “What is one thing you wish we did more of?” “Is there anything you have wanted to try but felt shy about?” Then wait. Silence after a question is thinking. Give them a minute. Do not fill it, do not rephrase it, do not answer it for them.

And when they do answer, believe them. If they say something that surprises you, that is the conversation working. If they say they are happy and you are not, that is useful information too — it tells you the gap is real and you need to say more about what you want. Listening is how you find out what your partner actually wants, instead of guessing.

When they go quiet

Sometimes you say something honest and the other person goes quiet. That silence can feel awful. You probably have not ruined anything. Quiet usually means they are thinking, or they are embarrassed, or they need a minute to find their own words. Do not chase them. Do not say “well?” and do not take the silence back with a joke. Let it sit. If they need to come back to it tomorrow, let them. Say “we don’t have to finish this now” and mean it.

If they shut it down completely, that is different — and it is worth noticing. A partner who refuses to talk about sex at all, every time, is telling you something. It might be shame, or a bad experience, or just never having had the words. Be patient, but do not drop it forever. Try again in a few days, smaller and gentler. And if it keeps hitting a wall, that is what a couples counsellor is for. Asking for help is what grown-ups do when a conversation keeps getting stuck.

Questions people ask

What if they laugh it off? Then say, gently, that you are serious. “I know it sounds funny, but I actually mean it.” A laugh is often nerves. Give them a second chance to hear you. If they keep laughing it off every time, say that too — “when I try to talk about this and you laugh, I feel like it doesn’t matter to you.”

What if we want completely different things? That is more common than people admit, and it is not automatically a disaster. Different does not mean incompatible. It means you need to talk about what each of you actually wants, and find the middle — the things you both enjoy, the things one of you is happy to try, and the things that are genuinely off the table.

How often should we talk about sex? Not every week, and not never. A short, honest check-in every few months is plenty for most couples. The goal is that neither of you is sitting on something unsaid for a year.
